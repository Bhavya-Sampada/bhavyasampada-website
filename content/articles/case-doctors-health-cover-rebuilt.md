*Names changed; shared with the client's consent.*

Dr. Madhavi had health insurance, and that was the problem: because she had it, she had not looked at it in twelve years. A ₹5 lakh family floater bought in 2014 was covering her, her husband, two teenagers and both her parents in 2026. We rebuilt it in stages, separating her parents onto their own plan, raising the family base cover and adding a super top-up. This case shows why a policy that exists is not the same as a policy that works.

## Where they started

Dr. Madhavi, 44, is a radiologist at a private hospital in Visakhapatnam. Her husband runs a small business. Their children are 16 and 13. Her parents, both in their early seventies, live with them.

She had bought a ₹5 lakh family floater in 2014 and added her parents a few years later when their old employer cover lapsed. The premium climbed every year and she assumed that was normal.

She came to us about her mutual fund SIPs. Insurance came up only because we ask every client to bring every policy to the first review. She brought a folder she had not opened in years.

## What we found

The sum insured had not moved since 2014. A ₹5 lakh floater shared across six people, two of them in their seventies, could be exhausted by a single stay in a corporate hospital today.

The parents were driving the premium. Because the floater priced itself on the oldest member, Madhavi's family of four was effectively paying senior-citizen rates for everybody. Every year the premium rose, and every year the cover stayed flat.

The policy had a room-rent cap of 1% of the sum insured per day, or ₹5,000. A private room at her own hospital costs more, and when it does, the insurer proportionately reduces the whole claim, not just the room charge. She had seen this happen to patients. She had not checked her own policy.

The waiting period for her father's pre-existing conditions had been served long ago, which was good. But any new policy for the parents would restart some waiting periods, so timing mattered.

The nominee was her late father-in-law. She had no top-up of any kind.

> Check three things on your health policy today: the sum insured, the room-rent cap, and the nominee. Each takes a minute, and each can decide a claim.

## What we did

We did this over about two months, in a sequence chosen so that nobody was ever uncovered.

First, we arranged a separate senior-citizen plan for her parents through portability rather than a fresh policy, so that the waiting credit from 2014 carried over as far as the rules allowed. Only once that policy was issued did we remove the parents from the floater at its next renewal.

Second, at the same renewal we raised the family floater from ₹5 lakh to ₹15 lakh for the four of them. With the parents off, the premium for the higher cover was lower than what she had been paying for ₹5 lakh with six members.

Third, we added a ₹50 lakh super top-up with a deductible matched to the base cover. It pays once total bills in a year cross the deductible, and costs far less than raising the base cover to the same number.

Fourth, we chose a base plan with no room-rent cap. For a doctor's family, this was non-negotiable.

Fifth, we updated nominees on every policy, including her term plan, which also had the old nominee.

Sixth, we wrote it all on one page: who is covered under what, the deductible, renewal dates and the claims helpline.

## What changed

The family of four now has ₹15 lakh of base cover and a ₹50 lakh super top-up above it. The parents have their own plan with their waiting credit carried over. There is no room-rent cap on the family plan. The nominees are correct.

Her combined annual premium went up modestly, far less than the increase in cover.

No claim has been needed yet, and we hope none is for a long time. Cover is rebuilt before it is used, because it cannot be rebuilt during a hospital stay. She kept her SIPs where they were.

## What you can take from this

If you bought a health policy before 2018 and have not changed the sum insured, it is almost certainly too small now. Hospital costs have moved; your cover has not.

If your parents are on your family floater, ask what the premium would be without them, and what a separate senior plan would cost. The answer is often surprising.

If your policy has a room-rent cap, find out what a room costs at the hospital you would actually go to. If the room costs more than the cap, your claim will be cut proportionately.

Ask for portability rather than a fresh policy when you move parents or yourself, so that waiting periods served are not lost.

Premiums for yourself and your parents can qualify under Section 80D in the old tax regime; check the current year's rules. Nobody should buy cover for the deduction, but it is worth knowing.

And check your nominee.

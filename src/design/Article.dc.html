You need roughly 25 times your expected annual spending in the first year of retirement, built from today's monthly budget rather than a round number someone quoted at a party. For most private-sector families that works out to a larger figure than they expect, because there is no pension to fall back on. This guide shows how to build your own number step by step, where the 25x rule breaks, and how EPF, NPS and PPF fit into the picture.

Start with what you spend today

Do not begin with a target corpus. Begin with your bank statement. Add up what your household actually spends in a normal month: groceries, utilities, fuel, help at home, eating out, subscriptions, gifts, travel averaged across the year. Most families underestimate this the first time, so use three months of statements, not memory.

Then adjust it for retirement. Some costs stop. Home-loan EMIs will usually be cleared by then. Children's school and college fees end. Commuting shrinks. Other costs grow. Health insurance premiums rise steeply after 60, and out-of-pocket medical spending rises with them. You may travel more in the first decade and need help at home later.

The result is your "retirement monthly spend in today's money". Write it down. Everything else builds on it.

Account for inflation honestly

Prices in 2046 will not be prices in 2026. You need an inflation assumption, and it should be modest and clearly labelled as an assumption. We usually assume 6 percent for general spending and a higher figure for medical costs, because hospital bills have risen faster than the overall index for years. Revisit the assumption every few years; it is a guess, not a fact.

Here is the arithmetic. Say your retirement monthly spend today is ₹60,000 and you are 20 years from retiring. At an assumed 6 percent a year, ₹60,000 today becomes a little over ₹1.9 lakh a month in 20 years. That is roughly ₹23 lakh a year in your first retirement year. This is illustrative; your own numbers will differ.

The 25x rule and where it breaks

Multiply that first-year annual spend by 25 and you have a rough corpus. In the example above, ₹23 lakh times 25 is about ₹5.75 crore. The rule comes from the idea that drawing a small, inflation-adjusted slice of a balanced corpus each year gives it a reasonable chance of lasting about 30 years. It is a thumb rule, not a promise, and it has three well-known gaps for Indian families.

  • Longevity: if you retire at 58 and live to 90, that is 32 years, longer than the rule assumes.
  • Medical shocks: one major illness can consume a year's spending in a month.
  • No pension: most private-sector people have no indexed pension, so the corpus has to do all the work.

Government employees on the old pension scheme are in a different position and can often plan for a smaller corpus. Everyone else should treat 25x as the floor of the range, not the ceiling. Some planners use 30x for a longer or less certain retirement; we would rather you aim slightly high and reach it slowly than aim low and run short at 80.

Worth knowing

Your retirement number is 25 times what you will spend in your first retired year, and that spend is today's budget inflated forward.

EPF, NPS and PPF are pieces, not the whole

Many salaried people assume their EPF will cover retirement. It is a strong, tax-efficient piece, and the employer contribution is money you should never leave on the table. But run the numbers: a mid-career salaried person's EPF at 58 is usually a fraction of the corpus above. NPS adds another piece, with the useful feature that part of it must be converted to an annuity, giving you some regular income. PPF is small but safe and useful for the debt portion.

Put all three on one page with your current balances and expected contributions. The gap between their likely total and your target is what mutual fund SIPs and other savings need to fill. Mutual fund investments carry market risk, and the gap may close faster or slower than you plan, which is why you review every year.

A worked example, clearly illustrative

Take a 40-year-old in Pune, salaried, planning to retire at 60. This is a made-up example to show the method only.

The honest answer is that neither method is better than the other; the right one depends on how your income arrives. A monthly salary suits a SIP, while a bonus, a professional fee or an inheritance is already a lump sum and needs a plan rather than a debate. This guide walks through when each fits, what matters more than the choice itself, and the two mistakes we see most often in reviews.

Start with how your money arrives

A SIP (systematic investment plan) puts a fixed amount into a mutual fund on a fixed date every month. A lump sum puts a larger amount in at once. People argue about which gives a better result, but your money already has a shape, and the method should follow it.

If you earn a salary, your money arrives monthly. Say ₹1.2 lakh lands on the 1st. A SIP on the 5th moves part of it into investments before it becomes spending. You never have a lump sum to debate about, and that is fine.

If you are a doctor with a clinic, a consultant, a contractor or a shop owner, your income is lumpy. Some months bring ₹4 lakh, some bring ₹40,000. A fixed monthly SIP feels like a burden in lean months and far too small in good ones. You need a different rhythm.

If you have just received a bonus, a retirement benefit, sale proceeds or an inheritance, the money is already sitting in your bank. The question is not SIP or lump sum. The question is what to do with it, and how fast.

SIP is really about discipline

The quiet strength of a SIP is not a mathematical edge. It is that the decision is made once and then repeats without you. No monthly willpower, no reading the news to decide whether this is a good month. For most salaried families, that automation is the single biggest reason their investments actually build up over a decade.

A SIP also buys units at many different prices over the years, which softens the discomfort of investing at a high point. That is a comfort, not a promise; markets can fall for long stretches and a SIP will not protect you from that.

Worth knowing

The right method is the one you will keep running for ten years without thinking about it.

Two habits help. Step up the SIP amount every time your salary rises, even by ₹1,000 or ₹2,000. And set the SIP date a few days after salary day, so it goes out before the card bill and the weekend plans do.

Lump sum needs a plan, not a formula

When a large amount arrives, the common advice is "stagger it over six or twelve months". That is a reasonable choice for someone who would lose sleep if the market fell the week after investing. It is a choice, not a rule. If the money is meant for a goal ten or fifteen years away and your asset mix already has a cushion, investing it sooner is equally defensible. Nobody can tell you in advance which path would have done better.

What we do insist on is a written plan before the money moves anywhere:

  • Clear any high-cost loan first, especially credit card dues or a personal loan.
  • Top up your emergency fund to six months of expenses if it is short.
  • Decide what goal this money serves and how far away that goal is.
  • Only then choose the mix of equity and debt, and how quickly to deploy.

For lumpy professional income, a simple version works well. Keep a floor SIP you can afford even in a poor month, and move a fixed share of every good month's surplus into investments as a lump sum. The habit stays, the amount flexes.

What matters more than SIP vs lump sum

Over fifteen years, the method of entry makes a small difference. Three other things make a large one.

FactorWhy it matters more
The goalA child's college fund in six years and your retirement in twenty need different approaches
The horizonMoney needed within three years should not be in equity at all
The asset mixHow much is in equity, debt, gold and property shapes your experience far more than the entry date

Most families who come to us for a first review have never written down the goal behind their investments. Once they do, the SIP versus lump sum question usually answers itself.

The two mistakes to avoid

The first is stopping SIPs when markets fall. A fall is uncomfortable, but a SIP that pauses in a downturn skips the very months it was designed for. If cash is tight, reduce the amount; do not stop it.

The second is waiting for the "right time" with a lump sum. We meet people who have held ₹20 lakh in a savings account for two years waiting for a correction. Inflation does not wait. If you cannot bring yourself to invest it all, stagger it on a fixed schedule and stop watching the index.

Both mistakes come from trying to time the market instead of running a plan. Mutual fund investments carry market risk, and no method removes that risk. A plan simply makes sure the risk you take matches the goal you have.

What to do this week

  • Write down every goal you are investing for, with a year next to each one.
  • Check your SIP date and move it to within five days of salary day.
  • If any lump sum is sitting idle, list what it is for and when you need it.

Ravi and Sunitha came to us asking about a home-loan balance transfer, and left with something simpler and cheaper: a rate reset with their own lender, a part-prepayment from money that was sitting idle, and a shorter tenure. Their EMI went down, the loan got shorter, and their retirement SIP never stopped. This case walks through how we got there and why the obvious option was not the right one.

Where they started

Ravi, 38, works at a public sector bank in Warangal. Sunitha, 35, teaches at a private school. They have one daughter in primary school and a home loan taken in 2023 with about 17 years left to run.

A colleague had told Ravi that a balance transfer could lower his rate. He had collected two quotes. Both new lenders were happy to extend the tenure back to 20 years to make the monthly number look smaller.

Both had a term plan. Ravi had a retirement SIP of ₹12,000 a month, started three years earlier. Sunitha held a fixed deposit of about ₹6 lakh that had matured and was auto-renewing, with no goal attached to it.

Their question was narrow: which transfer offer should we take? We asked to look at the whole loan first.

What we found

Three things stood out when we read the loan statement and the two quotes side by side.

First, Ravi's loan was on an older benchmark with a spread that had not been reviewed since the loan started. His own lender offered a rate-reset facility for a one-time fee. Nobody had told him this was an option. The reset would bring his rate close to what the new lenders were quoting, without the paperwork, the fresh legal and valuation charges, or the processing fee of a transfer.

Second, both transfer quotes quietly stretched the tenure. A 20-year loan at a slightly lower rate looks cheaper per month, but the total interest paid over the life of the loan goes up, not down. The lower EMI was mostly the longer tenure talking, not the rate.

Third, Sunitha's fixed deposit had no goal, their emergency fund was already in place elsewhere, and it earned less than the loan cost them. Using part of it as a prepayment was the cleanest win on the table.

Worth knowing

Before you move a loan to a new lender, ask your current lender for a rate reset. It is often the same result at a fraction of the cost and effort.

What we did

We laid out three options in a one-page comparison: transfer as quoted, reset with the current lender and keep the EMI, or reset plus prepay plus shorten the tenure. For each we showed the monthly EMI, the years left, and the one-time costs.

Ravi chose the third option. Here is what happened in order.

  • He applied for the rate reset with his own lender and paid the one-time fee. This took two visits and one week.
  • Sunitha broke the fixed deposit at renewal and they made a part-prepayment of ₹5 lakh, keeping ₹1 lakh aside as a buffer.
  • Instead of taking the lower EMI the reset allowed, they asked the lender to apply most of the benefit to tenure. They settled on an EMI that was lower than before but not as low as it could have gone.
  • We confirmed in writing that the loan had no prepayment penalty.
  • The retirement SIP of ₹12,000 stayed exactly as it was. We did not touch it, and we did not suggest pausing it to prepay faster.

What changed

Say the original EMI was ₹38,400 a month with 17 years remaining. After the reset and the prepayment, the EMI came down to about ₹34,300, a reduction of roughly ₹4,100 a month, and the tenure shortened to about 13 years. These are illustrative figures from their case; your numbers will differ with your rate and balance.

They avoided the transfer processing fee, legal charges and a fresh set of documents.

The ₹4,100 a month they freed up went half into a new SIP for their daughter's higher education and half into a bigger emergency fund, since Sunitha's school income is less predictable than Ravi's.

Nothing about their retirement plan changed. That was the one thing we were firm on.

What you can take from this

A balance transfer is a tool, not a reflex. It makes sense when your current lender refuses to reset your rate or when the gap is large even after reset fees. It often does not make sense when the new lender's lower EMI depends on a longer tenure.

A few questions to ask before you move a loan:

  • Has my current lender been asked for a rate reset, and what does it cost?
  • Does the new EMI come from a lower rate or from a longer tenure?
  • Is there idle money, like a matured fixed deposit with no goal, that could prepay part of the loan?
  • Would shortening the tenure serve me better than lowering the monthly payment?
  • Can I do all this without stopping my retirement SIP?

If you cannot answer the first question, start there. Ask your lender in writing; it costs nothing. Check the current year's rules on prepayment charges before you commit.

The biggest tax decision for a salaried or government employee is not which deduction to claim but which regime to file under; everything else follows from that. Under the old regime, five deductions do most of the work: Section 80C, 80D, HRA, home-loan interest and NPS. This guide walks through the regime choice first, then each deduction, then the one habit that quietly costs families more than it saves.

Step one: old regime or new?

For FY 2025-26 the new regime is the default. It has lower slab rates, a standard deduction of ₹75,000 for salaried people, and almost no other deductions. The old regime has higher slab rates but allows 80C, 80D, HRA, home-loan interest and more.

The honest split we see in reviews is simple. Many salaried people, especially those renting without a home loan or with few investments, pay less under the new regime. People who pay HRA-eligible rent, service a home loan and already fill 80C through EPF or GPF often pay less under the old regime. The only way to know is to run both calculations with your own figures. Your employer's payroll portal usually lets you compare; the income tax department's calculator does too.

One caution: with only salary income you can switch regimes each year, but the choice has to be told to your employer early, otherwise TDS runs under the default. Check the current year's rules before you decide.

Worth knowing

Run both regimes with your real numbers every April; the right answer can change when your rent, loan or salary changes.

Section 80C: the ₹1.5 lakh you may already be filling

Under the old regime, up to ₹1.5 lakh of eligible payments reduce your taxable income. For many employees it fills itself:

  • Your own EPF or GPF contribution, deducted from salary every month.
  • PPF deposits, if you have an account.
  • ELSS mutual funds, which carry market risk and a three-year lock-in.
  • Children's tuition fees for up to two children, within India.
  • The principal portion of your home-loan EMI.
  • Life insurance premiums, including a term plan.

Add these up before you buy anything new. A government employee with a decent GPF deduction and two school-going children is often already past ₹1.5 lakh by September. Anything bought after that saves no tax at all.

Section 80D: health premiums, including your parents'

Health insurance premiums for yourself, your spouse and children get a deduction of up to ₹25,000 a year under the old regime, and up to ₹50,000 if you are a senior citizen. Premiums you pay for your parents get a separate deduction of up to ₹25,000, or up to ₹50,000 if they are over 60. A small amount within these limits can cover preventive health check-ups.

This is one of the few deductions that pays twice: the policy protects your savings from a hospital bill, and the premium reduces your tax. Pay by any mode other than cash and keep the receipt.

HRA, home-loan interest and NPS

If you live in a rented home and your salary includes house rent allowance, part of it is exempt under the old regime. The exempt amount is the lowest of three figures: the HRA you receive, rent paid minus 10 percent of basic salary, and 50 percent of basic in a metro or 40 percent elsewhere. Keep rent receipts, and if annual rent crosses ₹1 lakh, you need the landlord's PAN.

If you have a home loan on a self-occupied house, interest of up to ₹2 lakh a year is deductible under Section 24 in the old regime, in addition to the principal under 80C. A family with both HRA and a home loan in another city can claim both, and this combination is the most common reason the old regime wins.

NPS works through two different sections. Your own contribution gets an extra deduction of up to ₹50,000 under Section 80CCD(1B), over and above 80C, in the old regime only. The piece people miss is Section 80CCD(2): the employer's contribution to your NPS. This deduction is available in the new regime as well, up to a percentage of basic salary that is higher for central government employees than for private-sector ones. Government employees are usually already covered; private-sector employees often have to ask HR.

DeductionOld regimeNew regime
80C, 80D, HRA, home-loan interestYesNo
Own NPS 80CCD(1B)YesNo
Employer NPS 80CCD(2)YesYes
Standard deduction₹50,000₹75,000

Rules and limits change with each Budget, so treat the table as a map, not the territory, and check the current year's rules.

The March mistake

Every year in the last week of March we meet people who have just bought an insurance-savings product because someone told them it would "save tax". It does, in the narrow sense. It also locks them into years of premiums for a product they did not need and would not have chosen in October. The tax saved in year one is small; the premiums over fifteen years are not.

Protection and tax planning are separate decisions. Buy a term plan for protection and a health plan for hospital bills because your family needs them, in whichever month that is. Fill 80C with things you would want anyway: EPF, PPF, tuition, loan principal. Then decide the regime. Tax saving is a by-product of a good plan, never the reason for one.

What to do this week

  • Add up your EPF or GPF, tuition fees and home-loan principal to see how much of 80C is already filled.
  • Run your salary through both regimes on the income tax department's calculator.
  • Ask HR whether employer NPS contribution under 80CCD(2) is available to you.

Dr. Madhavi had health insurance, and that was the problem: because she had it, she had not looked at it in twelve years. A ₹5 lakh family floater bought in 2014 was covering her, her husband, two teenagers and both her parents in 2026. We rebuilt it in stages, separating her parents onto their own plan, raising the family base cover and adding a super top-up. This case shows why a policy that exists is not the same as a policy that works.

Where they started

Dr. Madhavi, 44, is a radiologist at a private hospital in Visakhapatnam. Her husband runs a small business. Their children are 16 and 13. Her parents, both in their early seventies, live with them.

She had bought a ₹5 lakh family floater in 2014 and added her parents a few years later when their old employer cover lapsed. The premium climbed every year and she assumed that was normal.

She came to us about her mutual fund SIPs. Insurance came up only because we ask every client to bring every policy to the first review. She brought a folder she had not opened in years.

What we found

The sum insured had not moved since 2014. A ₹5 lakh floater shared across six people, two of them in their seventies, could be exhausted by a single stay in a corporate hospital today.

The parents were driving the premium. Because the floater priced itself on the oldest member, Madhavi's family of four was effectively paying senior-citizen rates for everybody. Every year the premium rose, and every year the cover stayed flat.

The policy had a room-rent cap of 1% of the sum insured per day, or ₹5,000. A private room at her own hospital costs more, and when it does, the insurer proportionately reduces the whole claim, not just the room charge. She had seen this happen to patients. She had not checked her own policy.

The waiting period for her father's pre-existing conditions had been served long ago, which was good. But any new policy for the parents would restart some waiting periods, so timing mattered.

The nominee was her late father-in-law. She had no top-up of any kind.

Worth knowing

Check three things on your health policy today: the sum insured, the room-rent cap, and the nominee. Each takes a minute, and each can decide a claim.

What we did

We did this over about two months, in a sequence chosen so that nobody was ever uncovered.

First, we arranged a separate senior-citizen plan for her parents through portability rather than a fresh policy, so that the waiting credit from 2014 carried over as far as the rules allowed. Only once that policy was issued did we remove the parents from the floater at its next renewal.

Second, at the same renewal we raised the family floater from ₹5 lakh to ₹15 lakh for the four of them. With the parents off, the premium for the higher cover was lower than what she had been paying for ₹5 lakh with six members.

Third, we added a ₹50 lakh super top-up with a deductible matched to the base cover. It pays once total bills in a year cross the deductible, and costs far less than raising the base cover to the same number.

Fourth, we chose a base plan with no room-rent cap. For a doctor's family, this was non-negotiable.

Fifth, we updated nominees on every policy, including her term plan, which also had the old nominee.

Sixth, we wrote it all on one page: who is covered under what, the deductible, renewal dates and the claims helpline.

What changed

The family of four now has ₹15 lakh of base cover and a ₹50 lakh super top-up above it. The parents have their own plan with their waiting credit carried over. There is no room-rent cap on the family plan. The nominees are correct.

Her combined annual premium went up modestly, far less than the increase in cover.

No claim has been needed yet, and we hope none is for a long time. Cover is rebuilt before it is used, because it cannot be rebuilt during a hospital stay. She kept her SIPs where they were.

What you can take from this

If you bought a health policy before 2018 and have not changed the sum insured, it is almost certainly too small now. Hospital costs have moved; your cover has not.

If your parents are on your family floater, ask what the premium would be without them, and what a separate senior plan would cost. The answer is often surprising.

If your policy has a room-rent cap, find out what a room costs at the hospital you would actually go to. If the room costs more than the cap, your claim will be cut proportionately.

Ask for portability rather than a fresh policy when you move parents or yourself, so that waiting periods served are not lost.

Premiums for yourself and your parents can qualify under Section 80D in the old tax regime; check the current year's rules. Nobody should buy cover for the deduction, but it is worth knowing.

And check your nominee.

Buy your parents a separate health policy, not a place on your own family floater, and judge it on its terms before its price. For anyone over 60, the fine print on waiting periods, room rent and co-pay decides whether a claim is paid in full, in part, or not at all. This guide covers what to look for, how to stretch the cover with a super top-up, the tax deduction you get for paying, and what to do if a parent cannot be insured at all.

Why a separate policy, not the family floater

A family floater shares one sum insured among everyone on it, and its premium is set by the oldest member. Add a 68-year-old father to a floater with your spouse and two children and three things happen. The premium jumps for everyone. One hospital stay for him can use up the cover your children were relying on. And when he crosses the insurer's age limit, the whole policy may need restructuring.

A separate policy in the parents' names keeps their claims and their premium in their own box. It is almost always the cleaner choice, even when it looks more expensive on the first quote.

What to check before you compare premiums

Senior citizen plans differ far more in their conditions than in their price. Go through these in order:

  • Waiting period for pre-existing conditions: diabetes, hypertension and heart conditions are common at this age. Shorter is better.
  • Room-rent cap: if the cap is ₹5,000 a day and the hospital charges ₹8,000, many policies reduce the entire bill in proportion, not just the room charge.
  • Co-pay: a 20 percent co-pay means you pay a fifth of every claim. Lower or zero co-pay is worth a higher premium.
  • Sub-limits: caps on specific treatments such as cataract or knee replacement, exactly the procedures parents are likely to need.
  • Restoration: whether the sum insured refills after a claim in the same year.
  • Network hospitals near where they actually live, not near you. A cashless hospital in Secunderabad is no help to parents in Warangal.

Also check the renewal age. The policy should be renewable for life, and the insurer should not be able to drop them after a large claim.

Worth knowing

A ₹10 lakh policy with no room-rent cap and no co-pay is worth more than a ₹15 lakh policy with both.

Base plan plus super top-up

Senior premiums rise steeply with the sum insured, so a large single policy gets expensive. A common structure is a base plan of, say, ₹5 lakh combined with a super top-up of ₹15 or ₹20 lakh that starts paying once total claims in a year cross the base amount. The top-up is cheap relative to the cover it adds, because it rarely triggers.

The catch is that a super top-up has its own waiting periods and conditions. Read them separately. And make sure the deductible on the top-up matches the base plan's sum insured, or you will have a gap neither policy covers.

Section 80D, and disclosing everything

Under the old tax regime, the premium you pay for parents over 60 gets a deduction of up to ₹50,000 a year, separate from the limit for your own family. The policy should name a parent as the insured and you as the person paying; pay by any mode other than cash and keep the receipt. This deduction does not apply under the new regime, and limits change, so check the current year's rules.

The most common reason a senior citizen claim is rejected is not the illness. It is a condition that was not mentioned on the proposal form. Diabetes managed with one tablet, a stent from ten years ago, a thyroid condition: all of it goes on the form. Disclosure may raise the premium or add a waiting period. Non-disclosure can void the policy at the moment you need it most. We have seen families spend years fighting a claim over a blood-pressure tablet that was never declared.

Sit with your parents when they fill the form, attach the medical records, and keep a copy. The same applies to porting. If an existing policy has poor terms, you can port it to another insurer and carry over the waiting periods already served, but the new insurer will ask for full disclosure again.

If a parent cannot be insured

Sometimes an insurer declines a proposal because of a serious existing condition, or the premium is beyond reach. That is disappointing but not the end of the plan. Build a dedicated medical reserve fund in your parents' or your own name: a fixed deposit or a liquid mutual fund, kept separate from every other goal, topped up monthly. Say you set aside ₹10,000 a month; in a few years it is a meaningful cushion against a hospital stay. Pair it with any government or employer scheme they qualify for. Some retired government employees already have scheme cover; check before buying anything.

A reserve fund does not replace insurance, but a family with ₹4 lakh set aside is in a far better position than one hoping a claim will go through.

What to do this week

  • Pull your parents' current policy and note the room-rent cap, co-pay and waiting period on one line.
  • List the two hospitals nearest to where they live and check whether each is in the network.
  • Write down every medication and diagnosis they have, ready for a proposal form.

Your family needs enough term cover to replace your income for the years they would depend on it, clear every loan, fund the goals you have promised, and then subtract what you already own. For most earning parents that lands well above the 10x income figure quoted online, and the gap is usually the home loan. This guide gives you the working method, explains what the thumb rule misses, and covers the practical details of term, riders, nominees and disclosure.

The working method in four lines

A term plan pays a fixed sum to your family if you die during the policy term. The question is what that sum has to do. Build it in four parts, using illustrative numbers.

PartIllustrative figure
Income to replace: ₹1 lakh a month for 20 years₹2.4 crore
Loans to clear: home loan and car loan outstanding₹45 lakh
Goals to fund: two children's higher education, parents' care₹60 lakh
Less assets that can be used: EPF, investments, existing cover₹65 lakh

In this made-up example the family needs about ₹2.8 crore of cover. Income replacement is the largest piece: take your monthly take-home, remove what was spent on you alone, and multiply by the years until the youngest child is financially independent or your spouse's own retirement, whichever is longer. Round it up rather than down.

Do not count the family home as an asset unless the family would genuinely sell it. Do not count an employer group policy as reliable either; it ends the day the job does.

Why 10x to 15x is a starting point, not an answer

The common thumb rule says cover should be 10 to 15 times annual income. It is useful as a sanity check and nothing more. It misses three things.

  • Loans: a ₹1.5 lakh monthly earner with a ₹70 lakh home loan needs far more than someone earning the same with no loan.
  • Dependants: a 35-year-old with two toddlers and retired parents has a longer runway to cover than a 50-year-old with grown children.
  • Spouse's income: a working spouse with stable income reduces the income-replacement part, sometimes substantially.

Worth knowing

Cover the years your family would need your income, clear the loans, and fund the promises; that is the number.

Run the four-line method first, then see where 10x to 15x falls. If it is lower than your method, trust the method.

Cover till which age, and which riders

A term plan should run until the people who depend on you no longer do. For most, that is somewhere between 60 and 65: children settled, loans cleared, retirement corpus in place. A policy to 85 or 99 costs a lot more and mostly protects nobody, because by then the corpus should be doing the job. Choose a level cover that stays the same throughout. If you are 30 and want cover to 60, buy the 30-year term now rather than in five years; premiums rise with age and a new diagnosis in between can make cover harder to get.

A rider adds a benefit to the base plan for extra premium. Two are often worth a look. A waiver of premium rider keeps the policy alive if you become permanently disabled and cannot pay. A critical illness rider pays a lump sum on diagnosis of listed conditions, though a standalone critical illness policy usually gives wider cover for a similar cost.

Most others are better skipped. Accidental death benefit riders pay extra only if death is by accident, which does not change what your family needs. Return-of-premium options raise the premium sharply for a benefit you can replicate with a simple SIP. Keep the term plan simple and the premium low; put the saving into health cover and investments.

Nominee, MWP Act and disclosure

Name a nominee and keep it current. A term plan bought before marriage often still names a parent. Review it after every marriage, birth or death in the family.

If you have loans or run a business, consider buying the policy under the Married Women's Property Act, 1874. A policy under the MWP Act creates a trust for your wife and children, and the payout is protected from creditors and other claimants to your estate. It has to be chosen at the time of purchase and cannot be added later; ask for the MWP addendum on the proposal form.

Disclose everything on the proposal: smoking, alcohol, every diagnosis, every medication, your actual income and occupation. A term plan is a contract of good faith. Non-disclosure found at claim time is the most common reason a family is refused the money, and by then nothing can be fixed. A slightly higher premium today is a small price for a claim that is paid.

Why a term plan, not a savings policy, for protection

A term plan pays only on death and returns nothing if you survive the term. That is exactly why it is cheap enough to buy ₹2 crore of cover on a middle-class salary. A savings or endowment policy combines a small cover with a long-term savings component, and the cover is usually a fraction of what the family needs.

Keep the two jobs separate. Protection comes from a term plan. Savings and investment come from EPF, PPF, NPS and mutual fund SIPs, each chosen for its own purpose and each carrying its own risks. Mixing them tends to leave you under-insured and over-committed to a premium you cannot pause.

A home loan balance transfer saves money when the new rate is clearly lower, you still have many years left to run, and the fees are small next to the interest saved. If any of those three is missing, the transfer usually costs more than it returns. This guide walks through the arithmetic, the fees people forget, the cheaper first step most borrowers skip, and the top-up pitch that often comes with the offer.

How wide the rate gap needs to be

A transfer is a decision about interest you will not pay in future, not the interest you have already paid. So the question is simple: over the years left, does the lower rate save more than the move costs?

As a rough rule, a gap under 0.25 percentage points is rarely worth the paperwork. A gap of 0.50 or more, with ten or more years left, usually is. In between, do the sum.

Say your outstanding balance is ₹40 lakh, 15 years remain, and your rate is 9.25 per cent. A new lender offers 8.65 per cent. The difference in interest over the remaining tenure runs to several lakh rupees, which is well above typical fees. Now take the same gap on a ₹12 lakh balance with four years left. The saving shrinks to a few tens of thousands, and after fees it may be close to nothing.

Worth knowing

A balance transfer is worth doing only when the interest saved over the years left is clearly larger than every fee paid to move.

The fees people forget

Lenders list the headline rate in large type and the charges in small type. Before you sign, ask for every one of these in writing:

  • Processing fee at the new lender (often a percentage of the loan, sometimes capped)
  • Legal and technical valuation charges on the property
  • Stamp duty on the new mortgage deed, which varies by state
  • Foreclosure or switch charges at the old lender (floating-rate home loans to individuals usually have none, but confirm)
  • Fresh insurance the new lender may ask you to buy
  • Your own time: two to four weeks of document chasing

Add these up. If the total is more than one year of the interest you expect to save, think again.

Why remaining tenure matters so much

An EMI is front-loaded with interest. In the early years, most of each payment goes to interest; in the later years, most goes to principal. A lower rate works on the interest part. So the same rate cut saves far more in year three than in year sixteen.

Years leftRate gapRough verdict
15 or more0.50 or moreUsually worth it
8 to 150.50 or moreDo the sum, likely yes
Under 5AnyRarely worth it

If you are in the last third of your loan, a transfer is almost always the wrong tool. Part-prepayment, covered below, does more for less.

The simpler first step: ask your own lender

Most borrowers do not know that their current lender will often reset the rate for a small conversion fee, sometimes a flat ₹5,000 to ₹10,000, sometimes a small percentage of the balance. No new documents, no valuation, no new mortgage deed.

Walk into the branch, or write to the loan servicing desk, and say you have an offer from another lender at a lower rate. Ask what they can do. Many will match or come close. Even if they do not match fully, closing half the gap for a fraction of the fees is often the better outcome.

Also check which benchmark your loan is linked to. Older loans may still sit on an internal benchmark that moves slowly. Moving to a repo-linked rate with the same lender can be cheaper than moving lenders.

Prepay, and choose tenure over EMI

Any lump sum you put into the loan reduces principal, and the interest on that principal stops at once. When you prepay, the lender will ask whether you want a lower EMI or a shorter tenure. Choose shorter tenure. Keeping the EMI the same and cutting years off the loan saves far more interest than a lighter monthly payment.

One caution. Prepaying by stopping your retirement SIPs is a trade most families regret. Your home loan has a fixed end date; your retirement does not. Keep the SIPs that fund your retirement running, and prepay from bonuses, surplus income, or money that was sitting idle. If you must choose, keep the SIP and let the loan run its course.

Under the old tax regime, home loan interest and principal still carry deductions. Under the new regime, which is now the default, most of these are not available. Check the current year's rules before you let a tax angle drive the decision.

The top-up trap

A transfer offer often comes with a top-up loan at the same rate. It sounds convenient: a renovation, a car, a child's fees, all folded into one EMI.

Be careful. The top-up turns short-term spending into a 15-year debt. ₹5 lakh borrowed for a wedding and repaid over 15 years costs far more in interest than a shorter personal loan, even at a higher rate. If you need a top-up, keep its tenure short and separate in your own mind, and never take one simply because it was offered.

  • Ask your current lender, in writing, what it would cost to reset your rate to today's rate for new borrowers.
  • Write down every fee the new lender would charge, add them up, and compare the total to the interest saved over the years that remain.

Stop an SIP when the goal it was started for is reached or within two to three years, when the fund no longer fits your written plan, or when your income has taken a real hit. Do not stop it because the market fell, because a neighbour has a better idea, or because someone is pitching a new product. This guide separates the good reasons from the bad ones, explains when to pause rather than stop, and shows how a once-a-year review replaces reacting to the news.

The three good reasons

An SIP exists to serve a goal. The reasons to stop it come from the goal, not from the market.

The first is that the goal is close. If the money is for a house deposit in 2029 or a child's college fees in 2028, the last two to three years are not the time to leave the whole amount exposed to equity swings. The SIP has done its work. What happens next is covered below.

The second is that the fund no longer fits the plan. Perhaps its mandate changed, perhaps it has drifted into a category you did not choose, or perhaps your plan changed and the mix of equity and debt you need is different now. This is a planning decision, made calmly, after checking the written plan.

The third is an income shock. A job loss, a long illness, a business that has slowed. If the EMI, the rent and the school fees are under pressure, the SIP is the right thing to pause before the insurance premium or the emergency fund.

The three bad reasons

Markets fell. This is the most common reason people stop, and it is the one that costs the most. An SIP buys units every month. When prices fall, the same ₹10,000 buys more units. Stopping when prices are low means you bought at the high prices and skipped the low ones, which is the reverse of what the SIP was built to do.

A neighbour's tip. A colleague's cousin has a scheme, a stock, a plot of land, or a trading app. If your plan did not include it last year, a conversation at a wedding is not a reason to include it now.

A new product pitch. Someone calls about a scheme with a "limited window". Any product worth owning will still be there after you have slept on it and checked it against your plan.

Worth knowing

Stop an SIP for a reason that comes from your plan, never for a reason that comes from the news.

Pause versus stop

Most fund platforms let you pause an SIP for a few months without cancelling it. Use this when the reason is temporary: a medical bill, a gap between jobs, a quarter of thin business income. Pausing keeps the mandate alive and avoids the friction of starting again, which many people never get round to.

Stop outright only when the reason is permanent: the goal is reached, or the fund has been replaced in the plan. Even then, stopping the SIP is not the same as redeeming the money. The units you already hold stay invested until the plan says otherwise.

SituationPause or stop
Three months of tight cashPause
Goal is 2 to 3 years awayStop and start shifting
Fund no longer fits the planStop, redirect the SIP
Market has fallen sharplyNeither, continue

Moving money as a goal nears

When a goal is two to three years out, the task is to protect what has been built. The usual approach is to move the money in steps, not all at once, from equity funds into lower-volatility options such as debt funds or fixed deposits. Some families move a fixed slice every quarter over the final years. The point is that the amount you need on the date you need it should not depend on what the market does in the last six months.

Tax matters here. Redemptions from equity funds held more than a year are taxed differently from short-term ones, and the rules change. Check the current year's rules, or ask us to, before you set the schedule.

Review once a year, with a plan

Most SIP decisions go wrong because they are made in the moment: a headline, a message in a family group, a red number on an app. The fix is boring. Pick one month each year, sit down with the written plan, and ask four questions for each SIP:

  • Is the goal still the same, and is the date still the same?
  • Does this fund still fit the role it was given?
  • Has my income changed enough that the amount should go up or down?
  • Is the goal now within three years, so that it is time to start moving?

If the answers are no, no, no, no, the SIP continues and you close the file. If you have had a raise, raise the SIP. That is the whole review. Everything outside that one month is noise.

  • Check whether any goal is now within three years. If so, note it as the first thing to discuss at your review.
  • Set one calendar reminder, twelve months out, titled "SIP review", and do nothing else to the SIPs until then unless income changes.

Keep six months of essential expenses in an emergency fund, or twelve if your household has one income or you are self-employed. Hold it in a savings account and sweep fixed deposits, never in the same place as your long-term investments. This guide covers how to work out the number, how to build it in steps, where to park it, what counts as an emergency, and why this one fund quietly protects everything else in your plan.

How much is enough

Start with essential expenses, not total spending. Essential means what you must pay even if income stopped tomorrow: rent or EMI, groceries, utilities, school fees, insurance premiums, medicines, transport to work, and the minimum support you send to parents. Leave out eating out, holidays, new clothes and gadgets.

Say your essential monthly spend is ₹60,000. Six months is ₹3.6 lakh. If you are the only earner, or your income comes from a practice or a business, twelve months is ₹7.2 lakh. That sounds large. It is meant to. The number is what lets you say no to a bad job offer, or wait out a slow quarter, without touching anything else.

A few adjustments. If your spouse earns and could cover the essentials alone, six months is fine. If you are a government employee with high job security, six months is still the floor, because medical emergencies and family needs do not care about job security. If you are an NRI with dependents in India, keep a separate Indian-rupee fund here for them.

Build it in steps

Nobody fills a ₹7 lakh fund in a month. Set the first milestone at one month of essentials. Then three. Then six. Treat each as a goal in its own right.

Three ways to get there faster:

  • Direct every bonus, arrears or tax refund into the fund until the first three months are covered
  • Set a standing instruction on salary day so the transfer happens before you see the money
  • Pause new discretionary spending, not existing SIPs, until the first milestone is met

If you are starting from zero and also running SIPs, do not stop the SIPs. Instead, split new surplus between the two until the fund reaches three months, then let the fund take the larger share until it is complete.

Where to keep it

The fund has two jobs: be there, and be reachable within a day or two. Return is a distant third. So the money goes in places that are boring on purpose.

LayerWherePurpose
First monthSavings accountReachable the same day
Next two to three monthsSweep-in FDs linked to that accountA little more interest, still instant
The restShort-term FDs or a liquid mutual fundReachable in one to two working days

Keep it in an account that is not your daily spending account, so it does not blur into monthly money. And keep it far from your long-term investments. The moment the emergency fund sits in the same equity fund as your retirement money, a bad month in the market and a bad month at home can land together.

Worth knowing

An emergency fund is the only money you own whose job is to sit still.

What counts as an emergency

An emergency is a cost that is unexpected, necessary, and urgent. All three. A hospital bill that insurance has not yet settled. A job loss. A car repair you need to get to work. A parent's sudden medical travel.

What is not an emergency: a sale on a phone you wanted, a wedding gift, a holiday that "everyone is going on", a down payment on a car, a festival. These are planned expenses, even when they feel sudden. They belong in a separate short-term savings pot, not here.

The test is simple. Ask whether, if the fund did not exist, you would borrow for this. If the honest answer is no, it is not an emergency.

Refill it after use

Using the fund is not a failure. It is the fund doing its job. The failure is not refilling it. After any withdrawal, go back to the standing instruction and rebuild to the full number before you raise any SIP or make any new investment. If the withdrawal was large, give yourself a written timeline, say twelve months, and treat it like a loan repayment to yourself.

Why it protects everything else

Families without an emergency fund still face emergencies. They pay for them by stopping SIPs, breaking fixed deposits meant for a child's education, taking personal loans at high rates, or letting a term plan or health policy lapse because the premium came due in a bad month.

Each of these costs more than it looks. A lapsed term plan may not be reinstated on the same terms. A stopped SIP rarely restarts. A broken education FD is a conversation nobody wants to have with a 17-year-old. The emergency fund is the wall between a bad month and a bad decade. It is the least exciting part of a plan and the one we check first in every review.

  • Open a separate savings account, or pick an existing one you do not spend from, and set a standing instruction for salary day.
  • Move whatever you already have idle in your spending account above one month's needs into that account today.

A 20-year savings policy, whether endowment, money-back or unit-linked, gives you a modest life cover and a savings component whose workings are hard to see. It usually does neither job as well as a term plan and a mutual fund SIP would do them separately. This guide explains how to read the policy you hold, the three options open to you, and the one rule that must come before any change.

What these policies are

An endowment or money-back policy promises a sum assured on death, plus a maturity amount if you live to the end of the term. Part of each premium pays for the cover, part goes to the insurer's costs, and the rest is invested on your behalf. A unit-linked policy works similarly but shows you the investment as units, with charges deducted along the way.

There is nothing improper about these products. Insurers are regulated, the terms are disclosed, and for some families the forced discipline of a 20-year premium has real value. The issue is fit. Most families who hold one bought it for the life cover, and the life cover is the weakest part.

Why the cover is small

Life cover should replace your income for your family. A common rule is ten to fifteen times annual income. Say you earn ₹12 lakh a year; that points to a cover of ₹1.2 crore to ₹1.8 crore.

A savings policy with a ₹10 lakh sum assured might cost ₹50,000 a year. A term plan with ₹1 crore of cover for a healthy 35-year-old non-smoker typically costs a fraction of that, because every rupee buys cover and none is held back as savings. The savings policy's ₹10 lakh would cover a family for perhaps a year. That is the gap most people do not see until a review.

Why the savings part is opaque

With an SIP in a mutual fund, you can see the expense ratio, the holdings, and the value of your units every day. With a savings policy, the bonus declared each year, the charges deducted, and the final maturity value are harder to trace. The illustration you were shown at purchase used assumed rates, and the actual outcome depends on the insurer's declared bonuses over two decades. It may turn out fine. The point is that you cannot easily check, and what you cannot check you cannot plan around.

How to read your policy

Pull out the policy document and find these five numbers. If you cannot find them, the insurer's helpline must give them to you.

  • Annual premium and the number of years still to pay
  • Sum assured on death
  • Current surrender value, which is what you would receive if you stopped today
  • Paid-up value, which is the reduced sum assured if you stop paying but leave the policy to run
  • Maturity date and the projected maturity amount, with its assumptions

Worth knowing

Before you decide anything about a savings policy, find its surrender value and its paid-up value in writing.

Your three options

Each policy is different, and so is each family. These are the choices; the right one depends on your numbers.

OptionWhen it tends to fit
Continue to maturityFewer than three to four years left, or surrender value is far below premiums paid
Make it paid-upMany years left, surrender value is poor, but you want to stop premiums
Surrender and redirectEarly in the term, and the freed premium can fund a term plan plus an SIP

Continue when you are close to the end. In the final years the maturity amount is near, the loss from surrendering is largest, and the premiums left are few.

Make it paid-up when the surrender value is low but you do not want to keep paying. The policy stays in force with a reduced sum assured, you pay nothing further, and you collect the paid-up amount at maturity. This is often the quiet middle path.

Surrender when you are early in the term, the surrender value is tolerable, and the same premium would now buy a proper term plan and start a monthly SIP for the goal the policy was meant to serve. Under the old tax regime, there may be a clawback of earlier Section 80C deductions on early surrender, and the proceeds may be taxable depending on the premium-to-cover ratio. Check the current year's rules before you act.

Do the arithmetic for your own policy. Two families with the same product can reach opposite answers.

The rule that comes first

Never surrender or stop a savings policy before a term plan is in force. Not applied for, not under medical examination, but issued and the first premium paid. A gap of even a month without cover is a risk no saving is worth. Buy the term plan, wait for the policy document, and only then decide what to do with the old one.

The same applies to health. If the savings policy came with a rider for critical illness or accident, check whether you need a standalone health policy or super top-up before letting the rider go.

What to do this week

  • Find every savings-type policy in the house and write down the five numbers above for each one.
  • Work out your family's income-replacement need, ten to fifteen times annual income, and compare it with the total cover you actually hold.
  • Request a term plan quote for the gap, so you know what redirecting the premium would buy, before deciding anything about the old policy.

When Lakshmi's husband died suddenly at 54, the first thing she needed was not a plan; it was for someone to help with the paperwork and then to leave her alone for a while. We helped file the term-insurance claim and the EPF and gratuity forms, put the money somewhere safe for six months with no decisions attached, and only then sat down to build a written plan around her monthly needs, her daughter's wedding and her own health cover. This case is about what to do, and what not to do, in the months after a loss.

Where they started

Lakshmi, 52, lives in Secunderabad. Her husband had worked for a private engineering firm for 26 years. Their daughter is 26 and engaged; their son is 23 and working in Chennai.

Her husband had been a client of ours for some years. He had a term plan of a sensible size, EPF, gratuity due, some mutual fund holdings, a small fixed deposit, and a fully paid house. Lakshmi had never handled the family's money. She knew where the folder was, and that was all.

Within days of the funeral, two relatives had suggested where to "put the money" and a bank officer had called about a product. She called us because her husband had told her to, years ago, "if anything happens".

What we found

We asked for nothing in the first week except the death certificate and the folder. We made a list of what needed filing and by when.

The term plan nominee was Lakshmi, correctly recorded. That detail, checked years earlier at a review, made everything that followed simpler.

The EPF nomination was also in her name. Two older mutual fund folios had no nominee, which meant a transmission process with a legal heir certificate.

She had no health cover of her own; her husband's employer policy had ended with his employment. She did not know what her household spent in a month, and we did not ask yet.

Worth knowing

After a loss, the only financial decision that cannot wait is the paperwork. Every other decision can wait six months, and should.

What we did

In the first month, we helped file the term claim, the EPF withdrawal and EPS pension forms, and the gratuity form with the employer. We began the nominee transmission on the mutual fund folios and the legal heir process for the two without a nominee. Her son came from Chennai for a week to sign where needed.

We also arranged her own health cover that month, because that could not wait: an individual policy with a super top-up.

When the term claim was paid, about seven weeks after filing, and the EPF and gratuity followed, we put the money in a safe, liquid place that needed no decisions. We gave her a one-line note: "Nothing needs to be decided about this money until April." We asked her to show it to anyone who called with a suggestion.

Then we did not meet for several months.

In the sixth month, she asked to meet. We started with the question we had held back: what does the house spend in a month? She had kept a notebook for three months by then and knew. Say it was ₹48,000.

From that, over three meetings, we wrote a plan. It had four parts.

  • A monthly income arrangement: an amount set aside in a conservative option to produce a steady monthly transfer to her account, so that her bank balance behaves the way a salary used to.
  • Her daughter's wedding, eighteen months away: a sum set aside separately in a safe option.
  • A longer-term portion for her own later years, started gradually rather than in one go.
  • An emergency fund, separate from everything else.

Her son and daughter were in the room for the final meeting at her request, and both are now nominees on everything.

What changed

The term claim was settled. EPF, EPS and gratuity were received. All mutual fund folios are now in her name, with her children as nominees. She has her own health cover with a super top-up.

For six months, nothing was bought, nothing was invested, and nothing was decided. That was deliberate.

Now she has a written plan on three pages. Money arrives in her account every month in a predictable amount. Her daughter's wedding is funded and ring-fenced. She reviews the plan with us twice a year and can explain every line of it.

What you can take from this

Check the nominee on every policy, EPF account and mutual fund folio in your house this week. It is the single kindest thing you can do for the person who will have to handle things.

If you have just lost someone, let the paperwork be the only thing you do. Ask someone you trust to help file the claims. Then put the money somewhere safe and simple, and give yourself six months. Anyone who tells you a decision cannot wait is usually selling something.

Buy your own health cover early, even if an employer or a spouse's policy covers you today.

And if you are the one with the term plan, tell your family who to call. Write it in the folder.

Plan a child's education fund by naming the course, the year, and today's cost, then building towards that figure in a separate pot that never mixes with retirement money. Start early, move towards safer options as the date nears, and remember that a child can borrow for college but you cannot borrow for your retirement. This guide walks through the figure, the schedule, the separation, and the two conversations, one with your child and one with grandparents, that make the plan hold.

Put a date and a rupee figure on it

Vague goals do not get funded. "Something for the kids" becomes a number only when you answer three questions: which course, in which year, and what it costs today.

Say your daughter is eight and you expect her to start an engineering degree at eighteen. Today a four-year programme at a good private college might cost ₹12 lakh including hostel. Assume education costs rise at 8 per cent a year, which is higher than general inflation and has been for a long time. In ten years that ₹12 lakh becomes roughly ₹26 lakh. That is the goal, with a date: ten years from now.

If a postgraduate degree abroad is on the table, the figure is larger and the currency risk is real. Plan for the course you can see today, and revisit the figure every year. You will be wrong about the course. The date will not move.

Start early, because time is the main input

Every year you delay raises the monthly amount you need. A goal of ₹26 lakh in ten years needs a very different monthly SIP from the same goal in five years. Starting when the child is three instead of ten is the single biggest lever you have, bigger than any choice of fund.

If you are starting late, say with four years to go, accept that most of the money will come from your own savings and perhaps a partial education loan, not from growth. That is fine. A plan that is honest about this is better than one that hopes for a market rescue.

Keep it separate from retirement

The most common mistake we see in reviews is one large pot labelled "future", which is really retirement money that gets spent on college fees at 50, leaving a 15-year hole with no time to fill it.

Keep two separate folios or accounts, two SIPs, two goal names. The education SIP ends when the child enrols. The retirement SIP does not stop for anything. If money is tight and only one can be funded fully, fund retirement first and let the education fund be smaller.

Worth knowing

Your child can take an education loan. There is no such thing as a retirement loan.

This is not harsh. An education loan is repaid by a graduate with thirty working years ahead. A retirement shortfall is carried by a parent with none. Under the old tax regime, interest on an education loan carries a deduction under Section 80E; check the current year's rules.

Move to safer options as the date nears

Education money has a hard date. The fees are due in a particular month, and the college does not wait for the market to recover.

Years to goWhere the money tends to sit
More than 7Mostly equity funds via SIP
3 to 7Shift in steps towards a mix with debt funds
Under 3Largely debt funds, FDs, and the savings account

Make the shift in steps, say a fixed slice every six months, rather than one big move. By the time the admission letter arrives, the first year's fees should be sitting somewhere that does not fluctuate. Later years can stay partly invested, since they are not needed yet.

Involve the child at 15 or 16

By mid-teens, the course is coming into focus and so is the cost. Show your child the number. Not to burden them, but so that they understand what a seat costs, what the family has set aside, and what any gap looks like. A 16-year-old who knows the plan is far more likely to take entrance exams seriously, consider a scholarship, or take a part of an education loan with their eyes open.

It also removes the sense, common in Indian families, that money is a secret handled by one parent. The plan belongs to the whole family.

Grandparents' gifts

Grandparents often want to help, and their gifts can be a meaningful part of the fund. A few practical points. Gifts from a grandparent to a grandchild are generally not taxed in the grandchild's hands, but income earned on that money while the child is a minor is usually clubbed with a parent's income; check the current year's rules. Keep gifts in the child's own folio or account, in the child's name with a parent as guardian, so the money is clearly earmarked and does not drift into household spending. And ask grandparents to give a fixed amount on birthdays or festivals rather than one lump sum that nobody has planned for.

What to do this week

  • Write down the course, the year, and today's cost for each child. Apply a modest education-inflation assumption and note the target figure.
  • Check whether your education money and your retirement money sit in the same folio or account. If they do, open a second one this week.
  • If your child is 15 or older, show them the figure and the plan over a meal.
